# Retrospectives: Formats, Facilitation and Follow-Through

A retrospective is a structured meeting in which a team reflects on a recent period of work and decides how to improve. Retrospectives are a core event in Scrum, where the sprint retrospective closes each sprint. They are also widely used in other agile and non-agile settings, including project post-mortems, incident reviews and end-of-quarter reviews. The underlying idea, that teams should regularly reflect on how to become more effective and adjust their behaviour accordingly, is one of the principles behind the Agile Manifesto. This article describes the typical structure of a retrospective, common formats, facilitation practices, and how to make sure action items lead to real change.

## Purpose

Retrospectives focus on **how** the team works, not only **what** it delivered. Typical topics include collaboration, communication, tools, processes, quality, workload and morale. A good retrospective aims to:

- create a shared understanding of what happened;
- surface problems early, before they become entrenched;
- recognise what is going well so it can be kept;
- agree on a small number of concrete improvements;
- build trust and psychological safety within the team.

Retrospectives differ from status meetings and from sprint reviews. A sprint review inspects the product with stakeholders. A retrospective inspects the team's way of working.

## A Common Structure

A widely used five-stage structure, popular in the agile community, is:

1. **Set the stage.** Welcome participants, state the goal and time box, and run a short check-in. Many teams restate a "prime directive," a statement that everyone did the best they could given what they knew at the time, to encourage a blame-free tone.
2. **Gather data.** Collect facts and experiences from the period, such as events, metrics, feelings and observations, often using a timeline or a format such as those described below.
3. **Generate insights.** Look for patterns and root causes. Techniques include grouping similar items, "five whys," and fishbone diagrams.
4. **Decide what to do.** Select a small number of improvements and turn them into specific action items with owners.
5. **Close the retrospective.** Summarise decisions, thank participants, and optionally ask for quick feedback on the retrospective itself.

The time box depends on the length of the iteration. For a two-week sprint, 60 to 90 minutes is common.

## Popular Formats

Formats provide prompts for the data-gathering and insight stages. Changing formats from time to time can prevent the meeting from becoming stale, although some teams prefer a stable format.

### Start-Stop-Continue

Participants list:

- **Start:** things the team should begin doing.
- **Stop:** things that are not helping and should end.
- **Continue:** things that work well and should be kept.

It is simple and action-oriented, which suits new teams or short retrospectives. Its downside is that it can jump to solutions before the problems are understood.

### The 4Ls

Participants reflect on what they:

- **Liked:** positive aspects of the period.
- **Learned:** new knowledge or insights.
- **Lacked:** what was missing, such as information, resources or skills.
- **Longed for:** what they wish had been present.

The 4Ls format gives explicit space to learning and to unmet needs, and it encourages a mix of positive and critical reflection.

### Sailboat (or Speedboat)

The team draws a boat heading towards an island:

- **Island:** the team's goal or vision.
- **Wind:** forces pushing the team forward.
- **Anchors:** things slowing the team down.
- **Rocks:** risks ahead.

Sometimes **sun** is added for things that make the team happy. The visual metaphor can make it easier to discuss obstacles and risks without making them personal, and it works well for longer-term or project-level retrospectives.

### Other Formats

Other formats include **Mad-Sad-Glad**, which focuses on emotions; **timeline** retrospectives, which map events and energy levels over time, useful after long projects or incidents; **starfish**, with keep doing, more of, less of, stop and start; and **lean coffee**, where participants propose topics and vote on what to discuss.

## Facilitation

The facilitator's role is to guide the process, not to dominate the content. In Scrum the Scrum Master often facilitates, but rotating the facilitator, or bringing in someone from another team, can bring fresh perspectives and let the usual facilitator take part fully.

Good facilitation practices include:

- **Psychological safety.** Team members need to feel safe raising problems without fear of blame or retaliation. The presence of managers can affect openness. Teams handle this differently, for example by agreeing when managers attend.
- **Balanced participation.** Silent writing on sticky notes or digital boards before discussion, round-robin sharing and anonymous input help quieter members contribute and reduce groupthink.
- **Focus on systems, not individuals.** Problems are framed in terms of processes, information flow and conditions rather than personal failings.
- **Dot voting.** When many issues come up, participants vote to choose which to discuss in depth.
- **Time management.** A visible time box for each stage keeps the session focused.
- **Remote considerations.** For distributed teams, online whiteboards, cameras-on norms where appropriate, and shorter sessions help. Time zone differences may call for asynchronous input before the meeting.

Signs of an unhealthy retrospective include the same issues coming up repeatedly without change, a few voices dominating, discussion that turns into complaints about things outside the team's control, and declining attendance.

## Action Items and Follow-Through

A common criticism of retrospectives is that they produce discussion but not change. Practices that help include:

- **Fewer, smaller actions.** Committing to one to three improvements is usually more effective than a long list.
- **Specific and testable.** Each action should be concrete, for example "add a definition-of-ready checklist to backlog refinement," rather than vague, like "communicate better."
- **Clear ownership.** Each action has a named owner, even if the whole team is involved.
- **Visibility.** Actions are added to the team's backlog or board so they compete for capacity alongside other work.
- **Review at the next retrospective.** Starting each retrospective by reviewing previous actions builds accountability and shows whether changes worked.
- **Treat changes as experiments.** Framing an action as a hypothesis with a review date makes it easier to drop changes that do not help.

Some teams track simple metrics, such as cycle time, defect rates or survey-based team health, to judge whether improvements have the intended effect. These numbers should be read with care and not used to compare individuals.

## Evidence and Limitations

Retrospectives are widely recommended in practitioner literature, and surveys of agile teams commonly rank them among the most valuable practices. Rigorous empirical research on their effectiveness is limited, however. Studies of team learning and after-action reviews in other fields, such as the military, healthcare and aviation, generally support structured debriefs for improving performance. Their benefits seem to depend on psychological safety and on whether lessons are actually acted on.

## Summary

Retrospectives give teams a regular, structured opportunity to reflect on their way of working and decide on improvements. A typical session moves from setting the stage, through gathering data and generating insights, to deciding on actions and closing. Formats such as Start-Stop-Continue, the 4Ls and the sailboat offer different prompts for reflection. Effective facilitation depends on psychological safety and balanced participation. The value of a retrospective ultimately depends on following up on a small number of specific, owned action items.
