# Termination of Employment in Germany: An Overview

German employment law is generally regarded as protective of employees, particularly when it comes to dismissal. Termination of employment is governed by a combination of statutes, collective agreements, works agreements, individual contracts and a substantial body of case law from the labour courts. This article gives an overview of the main elements: the Protection Against Dismissal Act (Kündigungsschutzgesetz), statutory notice periods, the role of the works council, termination agreements (Aufhebungsvertrag), and common norms around severance.

This article is general information, not legal advice; anyone facing a dismissal or negotiating a termination should consult a qualified employment lawyer or their trade union.

## Formal Requirements for Termination

Under the German Civil Code (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, BGB), a notice of termination (Kündigung) must be in **written form** with a handwritten signature to be valid. Termination by email, text message or other electronic means is not sufficient. The notice takes effect when it is received by the other party, which is why proof of delivery matters in practice.

There are two main types of termination by the employer:

- **Ordinary termination (ordentliche Kündigung):** termination with the applicable notice period.
- **Extraordinary termination (außerordentliche Kündigung):** termination without notice for "good cause" (wichtiger Grund), where continuing the employment until the end of the notice period cannot reasonably be expected. It generally must be declared within two weeks of the employer learning of the relevant facts. Typical examples involve serious misconduct such as theft or fraud.

## Notice Periods

Section 622 BGB sets the statutory notice periods. The basic notice period is **four weeks to the 15th or to the end of a calendar month**. For termination by the employer, the period increases with length of service:

| Length of employment | Notice period (to end of month) |
|---|---|
| 2 years | 1 month |
| 5 years | 2 months |
| 8 years | 3 months |
| 10 years | 4 months |
| 12 years | 5 months |
| 15 years | 6 months |
| 20 years | 7 months |

During an agreed **probationary period** of up to six months, the notice period can be shortened to two weeks. Employment contracts can provide for longer notice periods. The notice period for the employee may not be longer than the one for the employer. Collective bargaining agreements may set different periods, including shorter ones.

The statute contains a provision that excludes periods of employment before age 25 from the length-of-service calculation. Courts have held this rule to be incompatible with EU anti-discrimination law, so it is not applied.

## The Protection Against Dismissal Act (Kündigungsschutzgesetz)

The Kündigungsschutzgesetz (KSchG) is the core of German dismissal protection. It generally applies when:

- the employee has been employed for **more than six months** without interruption (the waiting period); and
- the business regularly employs **more than ten employees**, counted under specific rules where part-time employees count proportionally. Different thresholds apply to employees whose employment began before 2004.

Where the KSchG applies, an ordinary dismissal must be **socially justified**. It must rest on one of three grounds:

1. **Person-related reasons (personenbedingt):** the employee is no longer able to perform the work, for example because of long-term illness with a negative prognosis and significant operational impact.
2. **Conduct-related reasons (verhaltensbedingt):** the employee has breached contractual duties. As a rule, a prior **warning (Abmahnung)** for similar conduct is needed before dismissal, except in serious cases.
3. **Operational reasons (betriebsbedingt):** the job no longer exists because of business decisions such as restructuring. The employer must also carry out a **social selection (Sozialauswahl)** among comparable employees, taking into account length of service, age, maintenance obligations and severe disability.

Courts also apply the principle of proportionality. Dismissal must be the last resort, and alternatives such as transfer to another vacant position must be considered.

An employee who wants to challenge a dismissal must generally file a claim with the labour court (Arbeitsgericht) **within three weeks** of receiving the written notice. If this deadline is missed, the dismissal is generally treated as valid, apart from narrow exceptions.

Outside the KSchG, for example in small businesses or during the first six months, dismissal is easier but not unrestricted. It must still not be discriminatory, abusive or in bad faith.

## Special Protection

Certain groups have enhanced protection against dismissal. Examples include:

- pregnant employees and those on maternity protection;
- employees on parental leave (Elternzeit);
- severely disabled employees, whose dismissal generally requires prior approval from the Integration Office (Integrationsamt);
- works council members, whose ordinary dismissal is generally excluded;
- in some cases, data protection officers and employees caring for relatives.

## The Role of the Works Council (Betriebsrat)

Employees in businesses with at least five permanent employees eligible to vote may elect a works council under the Works Constitution Act (Betriebsverfassungsgesetz). Where a works council exists, the employer must **consult it before every dismissal** (Section 102). The employer must share the reasons for the dismissal. A dismissal made without proper consultation is invalid.

The works council can raise concerns or object on certain statutory grounds. An objection does not stop the dismissal, but it can give the employee a right to continued employment while the court case is pending. For larger restructurings, the works council has further rights, including negotiating a **reconciliation of interests (Interessenausgleich)** and a **social plan (Sozialplan)**, which often includes severance payments. Mass dismissals above certain thresholds must also be reported to the Federal Employment Agency.

## Termination Agreements (Aufhebungsvertrag)

A termination agreement, Aufhebungsvertrag, ends the employment relationship by mutual consent rather than by unilateral notice. Like a notice of termination, it must be in written form. Because it is not a dismissal, the KSchG, notice periods and works council consultation do not apply in the same way. This makes it attractive to employers seeking certainty.

Typical contents include the termination date, a severance payment, garden leave, handling of remaining holiday, a reference letter (Arbeitszeugnis) and confidentiality clauses.

Employees should be aware of potential drawbacks. In particular, the Federal Employment Agency may impose a **blocking period (Sperrzeit)** on unemployment benefits, often up to twelve weeks, if the employee is considered to have ended the employment without good reason. Whether a blocking period applies depends on the circumstances, for example whether a dismissal was otherwise imminent and lawful. Courts have also held that agreements must be negotiated fairly. An agreement may be challenged if, for example, it was obtained by unlawful threats or by violating the requirement of fair negotiation.

A related instrument is the **settlement agreement (Abwicklungsvertrag)**, concluded after a dismissal, and the court settlement (Vergleich) reached during labour court proceedings.

## Severance (Abfindung)

There is **no general statutory right to severance** in Germany. Severance usually comes from negotiation, a social plan, a collective agreement or a court settlement. In practice, many dismissal disputes end with a settlement in which the employee leaves in exchange for a payment.

A widely used **rule of thumb** is **0.5 gross monthly salaries per year of service**. This figure appears in Section 1a KSchG, which allows an employer to offer severance at that rate in return for the employee not challenging an operational dismissal. In negotiations, the actual amount often differs from the rule of thumb, from below 0.5 to well above 1.0 monthly salaries per year. The amount depends on how strong the dismissal grounds are, the risk of litigation for both sides, length of service, the employee's age and job prospects, special protection, and the employer's interest in a quick resolution.

Severance payments are generally subject to income tax, possibly with favourable treatment under certain conditions, but are usually exempt from social security contributions.

## Summary

Termination in Germany requires written form and must observe statutory or contractual notice periods that increase with length of service. Where the Kündigungsschutzgesetz applies, after six months of service in businesses with more than ten employees, dismissals must be socially justified on person-, conduct- or operational grounds, and must be challenged within three weeks. Works councils must be consulted before every dismissal. Termination agreements offer flexibility but may affect unemployment benefits. Severance is not a general legal entitlement but is common in practice, often negotiated around the rule of thumb of half a monthly salary per year of service.
