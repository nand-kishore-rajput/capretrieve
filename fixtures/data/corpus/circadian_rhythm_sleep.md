# Circadian Rhythm and Sleep: Architecture, Light, Caffeine and Sleep Hygiene

The circadian rhythm is the roughly 24-hour internal cycle that regulates sleep and wakefulness, body temperature, hormone release, digestion and many other physiological processes. Sleep itself has an internal structure, called sleep architecture, that changes across the night and over a lifetime. This article describes how the circadian system works, how sleep is organised, how light and caffeine affect both, and what the evidence says about common sleep hygiene advice.

This article is general information, not medical advice; persistent sleep problems should be assessed by a qualified clinician.

## The Circadian System

In mammals, the master circadian clock sits in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. Almost every tissue also has its own peripheral clock, and the SCN keeps these in sync. At the molecular level, the clock is driven by feedback loops of "clock genes" whose protein products switch off their own production over about 24 hours.

The internal clock does not run at exactly 24 hours, so it has to be reset each day by external cues called zeitgebers ("time givers"). Light is by far the strongest. Others include meal timing, physical activity, social schedules and temperature.

Sleep timing is commonly described with the **two-process model**:

- **Process S (sleep pressure).** Homeostatic sleep drive builds up the longer a person is awake and falls during sleep. Adenosine, a by-product of neural activity, is thought to be one contributor.
- **Process C (circadian).** The circadian system sends an alerting signal that rises through the day and drops in the late evening, which helps people stay awake despite rising sleep pressure.

Sleep comes most easily when sleep pressure is high and the circadian alerting signal is falling.

**Chronotype** refers to a person's natural preference for earlier or later timing. It is partly genetic. It also shifts with age: adolescents and young adults tend towards later timing, and older adults tend towards earlier timing. A mismatch between the internal clock and social or work schedules is sometimes called "social jetlag."

## Sleep Architecture

Sleep is divided into non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. NREM is further divided into three stages:

- **N1:** light, transitional sleep lasting a few minutes.
- **N2:** the largest share of total sleep, marked by sleep spindles and K-complexes on EEG.
- **N3:** slow-wave or "deep" sleep, with high-amplitude slow brain waves. It is linked to physical restoration and memory consolidation.

**REM sleep** brings vivid dreaming, rapid eye movements and temporary paralysis of most voluntary muscles. It is thought to support emotional processing and some kinds of memory.

These stages cycle about every 90 minutes, though the length varies. Slow-wave sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, and REM periods get longer towards morning. With age, the amount of slow-wave sleep declines and sleep becomes more fragmented.

Most adults function best on roughly seven to nine hours of sleep, but individual needs vary. Consistently short sleep is linked in observational research to higher risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, mood disorders and accidents. The direction of cause is not always clear, though experimental sleep restriction does impair attention, mood and glucose regulation.

## The Role of Light

Light reaches the SCN mainly through intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells. These cells contain the photopigment melanopsin and are most sensitive to short-wavelength (blue) light.

- **Morning light** tends to advance the clock, making it easier to fall asleep and wake earlier.
- **Evening and night light** tends to delay the clock and suppress melatonin, a hormone released in the evening that signals biological night.

Bright outdoor daylight is much more intense than typical indoor lighting. Getting daylight exposure, especially in the morning, is widely recommended to strengthen circadian rhythms.

The effect of evening screen use is debated. Laboratory studies show that bright, blue-rich light from screens can suppress melatonin and slightly delay sleep onset. Under real-world conditions, though, the measured effects on sleep onset are often small, around minutes rather than hours. The content and engagement of screen activity, plus the displacement of sleep time, may matter as much as the light. Evidence that blue-light-blocking glasses improve sleep is mixed and generally weak.

## Caffeine

Caffeine is the most widely used psychoactive substance in the world. It promotes wakefulness mainly by blocking adenosine receptors, which temporarily masks sleep pressure.

- The half-life of caffeine in healthy adults is commonly cited as about three to seven hours. It varies a lot with genetics, pregnancy, liver function, smoking and some medications.
- Caffeine taken within about six hours of bedtime has been shown in controlled studies to reduce total sleep time and slow-wave sleep, even when people do not notice an effect.
- Regular users develop partial tolerance, and stopping abruptly can cause headaches, fatigue and irritability for a few days.

Moderate caffeine intake is considered safe for most healthy adults. A common general rule is to avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening if sleep is a problem, but the ideal cut-off varies from person to person.

## Sleep Hygiene: What the Evidence Shows

"Sleep hygiene" refers to behavioural and environmental recommendations meant to promote good sleep. Common elements include:

- keeping a consistent sleep and wake schedule, including on weekends;
- making the bedroom dark, quiet and cool;
- limiting caffeine, nicotine and alcohol, especially later in the day;
- avoiding large meals and vigorous exercise close to bedtime;
- getting daytime light and physical activity;
- winding down before bed and keeping the bed mainly for sleep.

Each recommendation is plausible and several are backed by experimental data (for example, on caffeine, alcohol and light). However, sleep hygiene education **on its own** has only modest effects on chronic insomnia in clinical trials. Alcohol is a useful example. It may help people fall asleep faster, but it tends to fragment sleep and suppress REM sleep later in the night.

For chronic insomnia, **cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** is recommended by many clinical guidelines as first-line treatment. CBT-I includes sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring and sleep hygiene, and it has stronger and longer-lasting effects than sleep hygiene alone or sleep medication alone. Digital CBT-I programmes have also shown benefit, though effects vary.

The evidence on exercise is mostly favourable: regular physical activity is linked to better sleep quality. The older advice to avoid all evening exercise has been softened, because moderate evening exercise does not seem to impair sleep for most people. Very intense exercise close to bedtime may still delay sleep onset in some.

## Shift Work and Jet Lag

Night shift work and rapid travel across time zones throw the internal clock out of step with the external environment. Strategies include timed light exposure and light avoidance, planned naps, and in some cases low-dose melatonin taken at specific times. Long-term night shift work is associated with higher risks of several health problems. This is an active research area, and the size and causes of these associations are still being clarified.

## Summary

The circadian system, centred in the suprachiasmatic nucleus and reset mainly by light, interacts with homeostatic sleep pressure to set when people sleep. Sleep cycles through NREM and REM stages, with deep sleep early in the night and more REM towards morning. Morning light, consistent schedules and limiting late-day caffeine are well-supported ways to support healthy sleep. Sleep hygiene advice is sensible but has limited effects on chronic insomnia by itself, and CBT-I is the best-supported treatment. The real-world effect of evening screen light appears smaller than often claimed.
