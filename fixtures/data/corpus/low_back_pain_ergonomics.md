# Non-Specific Low Back Pain: Ergonomics, Exercise Therapy and Red Flags

Low back pain is one of the most common health complaints worldwide and a leading cause of years lived with disability. Most people have at least one episode during their lives. In the large majority of cases, no specific structural cause can be reliably identified, and the condition is called **non-specific low back pain**. This article explains what that term means, summarises evidence on workstation ergonomics and exercise therapy, and lists the warning signs that call for prompt medical assessment.

This article is general information, not medical advice; anyone with severe, persistent or worsening back pain should consult a qualified clinician.

## What "Non-Specific" Means

Low back pain is usually grouped into three broad categories:

1. **Non-specific low back pain:** pain not attributable to a recognisable specific pathology. This accounts for most cases seen in primary care.
2. **Radicular pain or radiculopathy:** pain radiating down the leg, often caused by nerve root irritation, for example from a disc herniation.
3. **Specific serious pathology:** rare causes such as fracture, infection, malignancy, inflammatory arthritis or cauda equina syndrome.

Pain is also classified by duration. Acute pain lasts less than six weeks, subacute pain lasts six to twelve weeks, and chronic pain lasts longer than twelve weeks.

Imaging findings such as disc degeneration, bulges and facet joint changes are common in people with **no** back pain, and they become more common with age. For this reason, many clinical guidelines advise against routine imaging for non-specific low back pain unless red flags are present or the results would change management. Early imaging without a clear indication has been linked to more interventions without better outcomes.

Many factors contribute to non-specific low back pain. Physical load, general fitness, sleep, stress, mood, beliefs about pain, and work satisfaction can all affect how likely pain is to occur and persist. This is often described as a biopsychosocial model.

## Natural History

Most acute episodes improve considerably within a few weeks, but recurrence is common. A minority of people go on to develop persistent pain. Factors associated with a higher risk of chronic pain include high initial pain intensity, fear of movement, catastrophising, depression and low job satisfaction. Purely mechanical findings tend to predict chronicity less well.

## Workstation Ergonomics

Office ergonomics aims to fit the workstation to the person. Commonly recommended features include:

- **Chair:** adjustable seat height so the feet rest flat on the floor or a footrest. Lumbar support that follows the natural curve of the lower back. Armrests that support the forearms without lifting the shoulders.
- **Desk and keyboard:** keyboard and mouse at a height that allows relaxed shoulders and elbows near 90 degrees, close enough to avoid reaching.
- **Monitor:** about an arm's length away, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. Laptops used for long periods are better paired with an external keyboard and a raised screen.
- **Movement:** regular changes of position and short breaks to stand or walk.

The evidence for ergonomic interventions **preventing** low back pain is weaker than often assumed. Systematic reviews of workplace ergonomic adjustments, lifting training and back belts have generally found little or no effect on preventing low back pain on their own. Ergonomic setup may still improve comfort and reduce aggravation for people who already have symptoms, and it is low-risk. The idea of a single "correct" sitting posture has also been challenged. Current thinking emphasises varying posture, since sustained static positions of any kind may be uncomfortable.

Sit-stand desks can reduce sitting time and some studies report modest reductions in back discomfort, but the evidence is of limited quality and long-term effects are uncertain.

## Exercise Therapy

Exercise is one of the best-supported treatments for **chronic** non-specific low back pain and is recommended in most clinical guidelines.

- **Effectiveness:** Systematic reviews find that exercise therapy produces small to moderate improvements in pain and function compared with usual care or no treatment.
- **Type of exercise:** Many forms have been studied, including motor control and core stability training, Pilates, yoga, general strengthening, aerobic exercise and McKenzie-based approaches. Head-to-head comparisons generally show **no clear superiority** of one type over the others. Choosing exercise the person enjoys and will continue may matter more than the specific method.
- **Supervision and individualisation:** Programmes that are supervised, individually tailored and include stretching and strengthening may produce somewhat larger effects.
- **Acute pain:** For acute low back pain, specific exercise therapy has shown little additional benefit over advice to stay active. Staying as active as possible, rather than resting in bed, is consistently recommended.

Other non-drug approaches with supporting evidence include education and reassurance, cognitive behavioural approaches, and combined physical and psychological programmes for people with persistent, disabling pain. Spinal manipulation, massage and acupuncture show small short-term effects in some analyses, and guideline recommendations on them vary.

## Medication and Other Treatments

For acute pain, guidelines often suggest non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, taking account of individual risks. The evidence for paracetamol (acetaminophen) in low back pain has been disappointing, and some guidelines no longer recommend it as a sole treatment. Opioids are generally discouraged for chronic non-specific low back pain because the benefits are small and the risks significant. Surgery is rarely indicated for non-specific low back pain and is mostly reserved for specific conditions such as severe nerve compression.

## Red Flags

Red flags are features that may point to serious underlying pathology and call for prompt medical evaluation. They include:

- **Cauda equina syndrome signs:** new difficulty passing urine or loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the saddle area (around the genitals and buttocks), or sexual dysfunction. These require **emergency** assessment.
- **Progressive neurological deficit:** worsening leg weakness, foot drop or widespread numbness.
- **Possible malignancy:** a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is constant, worse at night and not eased by rest.
- **Possible infection:** fever, recent infection, intravenous drug use or a weakened immune system.
- **Possible fracture:** significant trauma, or minor trauma in someone with osteoporosis or on long-term corticosteroids.
- **Inflammatory disease:** gradual onset before about age 45, morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, improvement with exercise rather than rest, and waking in the second half of the night.

Red flags on their own have limited diagnostic accuracy, and many people with one red flag have no serious disease. Clinicians use them together with overall clinical judgement.

## Summary

Non-specific low back pain is very common, usually improves, and often recurs. Its causes involve physical, psychological and social factors rather than a single structural lesion. Ergonomic workstation setup may improve comfort but has limited evidence for preventing back pain. Exercise therapy is a well-supported treatment for chronic pain, with no single type clearly superior, and staying active is advised for acute episodes. Imaging is not routinely needed. Red flags such as bladder or bowel changes, saddle numbness, progressive weakness, fever or unexplained weight loss call for urgent medical assessment.
