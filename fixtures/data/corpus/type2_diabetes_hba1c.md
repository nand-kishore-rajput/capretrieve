# Type 2 Diabetes and HbA1c: Diagnosis, Treatment Approaches and Monitoring

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic metabolic condition marked by raised blood glucose. It results from a combination of insulin resistance, where the body's tissues respond poorly to insulin, and a gradual decline in insulin secretion by the pancreas. It accounts for the large majority of diabetes cases worldwide, and its prevalence has risen sharply alongside rising obesity, ageing populations and more sedentary lifestyles. Glycated haemoglobin, known as HbA1c, is central to how type 2 diabetes is diagnosed and monitored. This article explains what HbA1c measures, how it is used, and the main treatment approaches at a general level.

This article is general information, not medical advice; diagnosis and treatment decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare professional.

## What HbA1c Measures

Haemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells. Glucose in the blood attaches to haemoglobin slowly and without enzymes, a process called glycation. The higher the average blood glucose, the greater the share of haemoglobin that becomes glycated.

Red blood cells live for about 120 days, so HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over roughly the previous two to three months, with recent weeks weighted more heavily. HbA1c is reported either as a percentage of total haemoglobin or in millimoles per mole (mmol/mol), the unit used in many countries.

Advantages of HbA1c include:

- no need for fasting;
- less day-to-day variability than single glucose measurements;
- a strong association with the risk of long-term diabetes complications.

Limitations include:

- it can be misleading in conditions that affect red blood cell lifespan or haemoglobin structure, such as some anaemias, haemoglobin variants, recent blood loss or transfusion, chronic kidney disease and pregnancy;
- it does not capture short-term fluctuations, hypoglycaemia or glucose variability;
- there are differences between some ethnic groups in the relationship between HbA1c and average glucose, and the clinical significance of these differences is debated.

## Diagnostic Thresholds

Widely used criteria, including those from major diabetes organisations and the World Health Organization, apply the following HbA1c cut-offs in non-pregnant adults:

| HbA1c (%) | HbA1c (mmol/mol) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Below 5.7 | Below 39 | Normal |
| 5.7 to 6.4 | 39 to 47 | Prediabetes (increased risk) |
| 6.5 or higher | 48 or higher | Diabetes |

Some organisations and countries use a slightly different prediabetes range. For example, some use 6.0 to 6.4 percent (42 to 47 mmol/mol) to define high risk. In people without symptoms, a diagnosis of diabetes usually needs confirmation by a repeat test or a second, different test.

Other diagnostic tests include:

- **Fasting plasma glucose:** 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or higher indicates diabetes; 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L (100 to 125 mg/dL) indicates impaired fasting glucose, though some bodies use 6.1 mmol/L as the lower bound.
- **Oral glucose tolerance test:** a two-hour value of 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or higher indicates diabetes.
- **Random plasma glucose:** 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or higher with classic symptoms such as thirst, frequent urination and unexplained weight loss.

Different tests do not always agree, and each picks out a somewhat different group of people.

## Prediabetes

Prediabetes means blood glucose is above normal but below the diabetes threshold. Not everyone with prediabetes goes on to develop diabetes, and some people return to normal glucose levels. Large randomised trials of structured lifestyle programmes, focused on modest weight loss of around five to seven percent and regular physical activity, have shown substantial reductions in progression to type 2 diabetes over several years. Metformin has also been shown to reduce progression in some groups, although less than intensive lifestyle change on average.

## General Treatment Approaches

Treatment of type 2 diabetes is individualised. It takes into account age, other medical conditions, risk of hypoglycaemia, body weight, cost and personal preference. The goals are to control symptoms, reduce the risk of complications, and manage cardiovascular risk factors such as blood pressure and cholesterol.

### Lifestyle

Lifestyle measures underpin all treatment. They include:

- dietary changes, with several eating patterns acceptable (such as Mediterranean-style, lower-carbohydrate and calorie-reduced diets) and no single diet clearly superior for everyone;
- regular physical activity, including both aerobic and resistance exercise;
- weight loss where appropriate. Trials have shown that substantial weight loss, for example through intensive low-calorie programmes, can lead to remission of type 2 diabetes in some people, especially early in the disease;
- stopping smoking, and attention to sleep and alcohol intake.

### Metformin

Metformin has long been the usual first-line medication in many guidelines. It mainly reduces glucose production by the liver. It is inexpensive, does not cause weight gain, and carries a low risk of hypoglycaemia when used alone. Common side effects are gastrointestinal. It needs caution or dose adjustment in reduced kidney function.

### Newer Drug Classes

Several newer classes have changed treatment considerably:

- **SGLT2 inhibitors** increase glucose excretion in the urine. Large outcome trials have shown benefits for heart failure and chronic kidney disease in suitable patients. Side effects include genital infections and, rarely, ketoacidosis.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists** enhance glucose-dependent insulin release, slow gastric emptying and reduce appetite, often leading to significant weight loss. Several have shown cardiovascular benefit in outcome trials. Gastrointestinal side effects are common.
- **Dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists** are a newer development showing large reductions in HbA1c and body weight in trials.
- **DPP-4 inhibitors** are oral agents with modest glucose-lowering effects and a neutral effect on weight.

Current guidelines increasingly recommend choosing medications based on other conditions as well as glucose. For example, SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists are favoured for people with established cardiovascular disease, heart failure or kidney disease, sometimes regardless of HbA1c.

### Older Agents and Insulin

Sulfonylureas are inexpensive and effective but carry a risk of hypoglycaemia and weight gain. Pioglitazone improves insulin sensitivity but can cause fluid retention. Insulin may be needed as the disease progresses or when glucose is very high.

## Monitoring

- **HbA1c** is typically checked every three months while treatment is being adjusted and about every six months once stable. A common general target is around 7 percent (53 mmol/mol). However, targets are individualised. Less strict targets may suit older or frail people or those at high risk of hypoglycaemia, and stricter targets may suit some younger people if they can be reached safely. Trials of very intensive glucose lowering in high-risk patients produced mixed results, including harm in one large trial, which is one reason targets are individualised.
- **Self-monitoring of blood glucose** and **continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)** are particularly useful for people on insulin or medications that can cause hypoglycaemia. CGM provides metrics such as "time in range."
- **Complication screening** includes regular eye examinations, kidney function and urine albumin tests, foot examinations, blood pressure and lipid checks.

## Summary

HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over about two to three months. Values of 5.7 to 6.4 percent indicate prediabetes and 6.5 percent or higher indicates diabetes, usually confirmed by repeat testing, although thresholds vary somewhat between organisations. Treatment combines lifestyle measures with medications such as metformin and newer classes like SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, chosen based on individual circumstances. Regular monitoring of HbA1c and screening for complications are central to long-term care.
